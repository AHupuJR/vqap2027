About the workshop

Meaningful, reliable, actionable quality assessment

Visual quality assessment is essential for evaluating the images and videos we capture, process, edit, and generate. Advances in computational imaging and generative models demand assessment methods that go beyond low-level fidelity and subjective score prediction to account for human perception, perceptual realism, semantic and physical plausibility, and task-specific requirements. However, existing methods often struggle to generalize across content and applications or capture the quality dimensions that matter in practice.

The Visual Quality Assessment in Practice (VQAP) workshop brings together researchers in perceptual quality, image and video processing, computational imaging, and generative models. Through invited talks, paper presentations, and a panel discussion, we aim to advance meaningful, reliable, and actionable quality assessment.

We are particularly interested in how quality should be defined and measured across different visual tasks, how assessment methods can better align with human judgments, and how they can account for the increasingly diverse outputs of modern vision systems.

Scope

Topics of interest

Submissions should address the challenges of defining, measuring, and validating visual quality across different content types and visual tasks. We welcome both methodological advances and application-oriented studies. Topics include, but are not limited to:

1

Perceptual quality modeling and subjective assessment

Objective quality metrics aligned with human visual perception, perceptual similarity, fidelity–realism trade-offs, human preferences, subjective evaluation methodologies, observer variability, psychovisual factors, and the construction of perceptually meaningful ground truth.

2

Generalization and robustness

Evaluating quality models under domain shifts, unseen distortions, new acquisition and display technologies, and changing viewing conditions, including cross-dataset and cross-application generalization.

3

Quality assessment for generative and AI-enhanced content

Evaluating restored, enhanced, edited, and generated images and videos, including perceptual realism, semantic correctness, physical plausibility, consistency with user intent, and temporal consistency of identities, objects, and scene dynamics.

4

Quality models for optimization and multimodal evaluation

Quality models as perceptual losses, rewards, and preference signals for image processing and generative models; data selection and filtering; robustness against metric over-optimization; and multimodal, explainable assessment with vision-language models and interpretable quality reports.

5

Quality assessment across visual applications

Computational photography, image restoration and enhancement, lighting estimation and relighting, image and video editing, compression and streaming, AR/VR/XR, 3D reconstruction, neural rendering and novel-view synthesis, and other emerging visual applications.

6

Evaluation methodologies and standardization

Reproducible evaluation protocols, benchmarking practices, subjective-study design, statistical analysis, and quality metrics supporting emerging image and video standards, with attention to interoperability and practical requirements.

We particularly encourage submissions that provide rigorous and reproducible evaluations, including details of subjective-study methodology, viewing conditions, statistical analysis, computational requirements, and failure cases. We also welcome work examining how quality assessment methods can guide the development, optimization, and evaluation of image and video systems. Reports of practical experience, including negative results, are encouraged when they provide evidence of where, why, and under what conditions a method succeeds or fails.

Motivation

Key challenges in visual quality assessment

Traditional quality metrics often emphasize pixel-level fidelity or perceptual similarity, while real-world assessment may also require judgments about visual realism, semantic correctness, physical plausibility, and consistency with user intent. Models that perform well on established benchmarks may not generalize to new content, processing pipelines, or generative paradigms.

Call for Papers

Submit your work

We invite submissions through two non-archival tracks. Accepted submissions from both tracks will be presented at the workshop but will not appear in the CVPR 2027 Workshops proceedings, allowing authors to submit expanded versions of their work to other venues.

Track 1

Published Work

Papers previously published or accepted at peer-reviewed venues. Submissions must cite the original publication, and their presentation must comply with the original publisher's copyright terms.

Track 2

Novel Work

Unpublished contributions submitted as full papers (up to 8 pages, excluding references) or extended abstracts (up to 2 pages, excluding references).

  • Submission: via OpenReview (link will be posted here when the call opens).
  • Presentation: we anticipate accepting four papers for spotlight presentations.
  • Review criteria: novelty, technical rigor, and relevance, with particular attention to the contribution to visual quality assessment and the connection to human perception, visual processing, or generative vision.
  • Ethics: authors are encouraged to report informed-consent procedures and participant compensation in subjective studies, and to respect image licenses, privacy requirements, and applicable data usage restrictions.
